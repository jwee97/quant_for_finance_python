# Company fundamentals: studying factors, searching for alphas, and counting honestly

A company's accounts say something about what its shares may earn next, but only some of that is real, and most of what looks real in a backtest is not. The **Fundamentals** tab keeps the accounts of listed companies from around the world in a local database, tests ideas built from them (one factor at a time, or by searching thousands of formulas), keeps every idea it tried (the failures too), and puts each survivor through the tests that are meant to catch the ones that only look good: dates the search never saw, a count of everything tried, costs, and a check that no figure was used before it was public.

The one rule behind all of it: **an interesting factor is not a validated alpha.** A result found in the sample it is measured on is a lead. The page says so in every verdict, and the status of an alpha in the library moves from *Candidate* to *Validated* only when the evidence survives the correction for the number of ideas tried (or a person approves it, with the reason on the record).

What this chapter covers: five minutes with the demo world; setting up real data (and what it costs); how the tab decides what was known when; what the factors are; how a study is read and how sure each number is; how the search works, what it keeps and how it is checked against luck; putting alphas together; testing an alpha as a portfolio; what was measured; and what is not known.

## Five minutes, with the demo world

No account is needed for this. The demo is a *simulated* world of companies with a few alphas planted in it, so the machinery has something it should find and the page can show you what finding it looks like. It is clearly marked as simulated on every view, it lives in its own file, and nothing found in it says anything about real markets.

1. Open the **Fundamentals** tab. At the top of the left panel, *Data* says **Real data** (empty for now) and **Demo (simulated)**. Choose **Demo**.
2. Open **Data manager** and press **Make the simulated world**. A hundred and twenty companies in six countries over fourteen years take about 35 seconds on a four-core machine with nothing else running (forty companies take about ten). From a terminal: `quant fundamentals simulate --demo`.
3. **Overview** now shows what the database holds (companies, statements, prices, how clean they are, what the library holds). Until a database is ready to study, the top of the page is a short list instead, *Not ready to study yet*: set up the key, add companies, fetch their statements, load their prices (and, on real data, whether there are enough companies to see a real signal). Each step is ticked when it is done, the first one that is not is marked *next*, and every step has the button that does it.
4. **Factor lab** → *Study this factor*. The first factor is free-cash-flow yield. The result is a verdict in words, six numbers (each with a 95% interval where one can be drawn), a table that says what a study of this size could have seen at all, the premium from a regression that holds size, beta, volatility and momentum fixed, a dozen charts (each with a table behind it) and breakdowns by country, sector and market regime. Open **Look-ahead audit** to see the same study run twice, once as it should be and once as if every figure were known at its period end, and then again with each figure used only 1, 30, 60 and 90 days after its filing. From a terminal: `quant fundamentals evaluate fcf_yield --demo --lookahead`.
5. **Alpha discovery** → *What could this search find?* says what a search of this size could find (free, a few seconds); then *Start the search*. Eighty formulas take about ten seconds. The bars are **standard** unless you choose others. Each formula is counted; most fall at the first tests. The leaderboard shows the best, none of them validated.
6. **Alpha library** shows the survivors (as *Candidates*) and, under **Graveyard**, every idea that failed and why. Tick two or three and open **Correlation**, **Combine**, **Model** or **Backtest**.
7. **Alpha monitor** asks whether each alpha still works on the data now in the database.
8. Back in **Alpha discovery**, *Calibrate on shuffled returns* (or *How much of this is luck?* on a finished run) runs the same search again on returns that have been dealt out to other companies at random, where nothing can be found, and says how much of what a real search turns up it would have turned up anyway (five shuffles of 200 formulas take about a minute). From a terminal: `quant fundamentals placebo --demo --n 200`.

## Setting up real data

The real database is filled from [OpenFilings](https://openfilings.org), a hosted service that returns each company's annual statements *as filed*, with the day each was filed, for about twenty-five markets. It is a deliberate choice: figures that were later restated are not what an investor could have seen, and the filing date is what a point-in-time study needs.

1. **Get a key.** Sign in at openfilings.org and create an API key in the dashboard (the examples in their documentation show keys that begin `of_`; the application does not depend on that).
2. **Put it in a secret, never in the page.** On Replit: *Tools → Secrets → New Secret*, name `OPENFILINGS_API_KEY_1`, paste the key as the value, press *Add Secret*, then **Stop** and **Run** (an app only sees the secrets that existed when it started). Outside Replit, set the environment variable the same way. Nothing in the page or the command line accepts a key, the key is sent in a header and never in an address, it is never printed or logged, and the database holds only a salted one-way hash of it (to tell keys apart in the usage counts).
3. **Test the connection.** *Data manager → Test the connection* spends one credit and tells the app which plan the account is on, which sets the allowance the quota manager enforces.
4. **Find a company and add it.** In the **Company explorer** type a name, a ticker (`us:NVDA`) or an ISIN. *Search my database* looks at the companies you already hold (an empty database says so, and the Country and Sector lists stay hidden until there is something to choose between). *Search OpenFilings (1 credit)* asks the provider's directory of listed companies: each hit shows the company, its market, country and ISIN, whether it is already in your database, and what fetching it would cost. **Add** is free and keeps the name and identifiers the search paid for; **Add and fetch** adds the company and fetches its statements for the cost written on the button. The same search again is answered from the saved answer for nothing (a month for a hit, a day for a search that found nothing). From a terminal: `quant fundamentals search nvidia`, then `quant fundamentals add us:NVDA`. If you already know the tickers, the Data manager adds a list for nothing: `us:AAPL jp:7203 uk:HSBA`, or `quant fundamentals add us:AAPL jp:7203`; then only the ticker and market are stored until the first fetch.
5. **Look at the plan, then fetch within a budget.** *Show the plan* says what is missing, what is already fresh, what it would cost and how many companies fit in today's allowance; `quant fundamentals plan`. *Fetch within the budget* will not start without a number of credits it may spend (`quant fundamentals sync --budget 100`), stops by itself when the budget or the allowance is reached, and can be stopped; what was fetched is kept.
6. **Load prices.** Yahoo Finance supplies daily prices (unadjusted and adjusted) and exchange rates; a market's usual suffix is tried first (`7203.T`, `HSBA.L`), London prices quoted in pence are divided by 100, and the symbol of a company can be set by hand. Companies that have delisted usually have no history there, which leaves them out of a study; the research context says how many. Once statements have been fetched, the Data manager offers to load their prices with one click (the checklist on the Overview says the same).

### What it costs

Every call is metered in **credits** against a daily and a monthly allowance. A search or lookup costs 1, a company's statements 10, so a new company costs about **11 credits** (plus 1 if it was found by a search). The price is per request and not per year: a series costs 10 credits whether it brings back three years or thirty, so the first fetch asks for a long history (from 2000, in one request). What comes back is whatever OpenFilings holds for that company, which can be far fewer years than were asked (see the next section); asking for years later costs another 10 credits, so it is worth asking for all the missing ones at once. The free plan is 100 credits a day and 1,000 a month: about nine companies a day, about ninety a month. A universe of 500 companies is about 5,500 credits, which on the free plan is more than five months. The application therefore keeps everything it downloads (the raw answer too, so a rule can change without asking again), asks again only for what is missing or has had a new filing since, and never refetches a company the provider said it did not know for two months. A failed call is refunded by the provider and costs nothing here.

### Why 11 credits can give only a few years, and how to ask for the rest

**What the 11 credits are.** A lookup of the company (1 credit) and a request for its statements (10). OpenFilings charges per request, and its pages say so: *financials series cost 10*, whatever the series holds ([rate limits](https://openfilings.org/docs/rate-limits), [pricing](https://openfilings.org/pricing)). The credits therefore do not buy a number of years. The first fetch asks for fiscal 2000 onwards in a single request, so the application is not what shortens the series; the years that come back are the years OpenFilings returned.

**Why they can be few.** Three things could cut a series short, and the documentation does not say which applies: OpenFilings may hold no statements for that company before some year (how far back it goes is not documented, and may differ between markets and companies); it may limit how long a series one request returns (its pages state no limit, and none for any plan); or entries in the answer may not be annual statements with figures, which this application leaves out. The application cannot tell the first two apart without being told, so it shows what it can see: the **company page** says, in words, what the last request asked for and what came back (*On 2026-10-20 the request to OpenFilings for FY2000 onwards brought back 6 annual statements (FY2019 to FY2024) and cost 10 credits ... It sent nothing for FY2000 to FY2018*), says when OpenFilings' own record of the request (its answer repeats the window it ran) differs from the window asked, says how many entries were left out and why, and keeps every request in a list (for a database made before the list existed it is read from the answers that were kept, which cost nothing to read). The raw answer of every request is kept, so what OpenFilings really sent can be read.

**The years you hold.** The company page has a calendar of fiscal years. Each year is *held* (a statement is in the database, and it is never asked for again), *missing* (nothing has covered it), *asked for, nothing sent* (a request covered it when its filing was due and the provider sent nothing), *due* (later than the latest year held, and its filing should exist by now) or *not due yet*. A year found empty is left out of the next request unless you pick it deliberately, and a newer year found empty is offered again after a month, since a late filing may have appeared. Quarterly and half-year statements held are listed under the calendar; OpenFilings' own cannot be requested yet (see below), so that table is read-only.

**Asking for the years you lack.** Click the years you want (Shift-click picks a run; *Pick every missing year* and *Pick what is due* are one click), and the page says what it would cost before anything is spent: *One request for FY2014 to FY2016: 10 credits (89 left today)*. Because the price is per request, the years you pick become **one request** from the earliest to the latest, not one per year, and a window that reaches a year newer than anything held is left open at the end so that a newer filing is brought along. Years in the window that you hold already come back in the answer and are left alone, never stored a second time and never written to; any other year in the window that comes back is stored, since it was paid for. The request will not start without the budget the plan showed. To test whether a series was cut short, ask for only the years before the earliest one held: a narrower request costs the same 10 credits, and a different answer from the first request's means the provider limits how long a series it returns. From a terminal: `quant fundamentals coverage us:NVDA`, then `quant fundamentals years us:NVDA --pick 2005-2012` (it prints the plan and asks for nothing) and the same with `--budget 10` to ask; `--ask-again` includes years that were found empty.

### Several keys, and what they cannot do

The page lists five key slots (`OPENFILINGS_API_KEY_1` to `_5`; a plain `OPENFILINGS_API_KEY`, a list in `OPENFILINGS_API_KEYS` and numbers up to 20 are read too), because several accounts might seem a way to five times the allowance. It is not, and the application does not pretend otherwise. OpenFilings' usage policy does not allow rotating accounts or API keys to get past its quotas, and several keys of one account share one allowance. So the **first working key does all the work**; a later key is a standby that takes over only if the first is rejected as invalid (revoked, say); when the allowance is spent the run stops, says when it resets, and waits. There is no switch that rotates keys. More credits come from a larger plan or prepaid top-ups (which never reset). The Data manager shows each slot, its role (*active* or *standby*), its state and what it has used, and a meter of the credits left today and this month (the Company explorer shows the same meter beside its search).

### What is not supported yet

- **Annual statements only.** The provider's documentation does not list the fiscal-period values for quarters or half-years, and a guess costs ten credits a try. A request for quarters is refused before a credit is spent. The trailing-twelve-month machinery exists and is tested on simulated quarterly data.
- **The sign of cash outflows is not documented**, so capital expenditure, dividends, buybacks, acquisitions and interest are read as magnitudes.
- Some filings in Japan and Korea are collected on demand: the first request may answer "still backfilling", which is treated as *ask again tomorrow*, not as a failure.
- **The adapter was written from the published documentation and tested against stand-in answers of the documented shape. It has not been run against a live key.** The first run with a key is the real test: fetch three or four companies and read the Data quality card.

## Two databases, never mixed

`data/user/fundamentals.db` holds the real data; `data/user/fundamentals_demo.db` holds the simulated world. The page and every command choose one (**Real data** or **Demo**, `--demo`), the demo never calls a provider, a simulated world is refused in a database that holds real companies, and an alpha found in one never reaches the library of the other. Both files are ignored by git.

## When a figure counts as known

This is the part most studies get wrong, so the application enforces it in one place (`src/fundamentals/pit.py`) and every panel is built through it.

- A figure is available on **the day it became public plus a lag you choose** (default one day, because a filing may come after the close), never on the last day of the period it describes: `available = filing date + information lag`. A restatement is a new version that is public from the day it was first seen, not from the original filing date; the original is kept beside it.
- When no filing date is known the application assumes a conservative lag after the period end for that kind of period, **flags the period as estimated**, and says how many periods needed that. A study can leave such periods out altogether.
- A study runs on a grid of rebalance dates (weekly, monthly or quarterly, the last trading day of the period). A figure appears on the first date on or after it became available and holds until the next filing; one that is too old expires instead of being carried for ever.
- The trade is made **a day after the signal** (default), never at the close the signal was computed from. Forward returns carry a company that delists before the horizon ends to its last price, so the losers stay in the sample.
- Anything learned from realised returns (a combination weight, a model fit, the sign of a formula) is used on a date only if the return window it learned from had ended by then: the *settled lag*, read off the grid, not assumed.
- **The look-ahead audit** runs a factor study twice, once as above and once as if every figure were known at its period end, and says whether the naive version flatters the result. On a simulated world in which net income is known a quarter early, the figure "predicts" returns when it is known early (IC above 0.04) and does not when it is known on time (below 0.01), and the audit says so. It also **waits**: the study is repeated with each figure used only 1, 30, 60 and 90 days after its filing, and the page says whether the IC falls to under half within a month (a signal that lives in the days after a filing is only as good as how fast it is acted on) or is still there after three (it is not about being first to read a filing).

## Missing data, bad data, and survivorship

- **A missing figure is never a zero.** A ratio is computed only when every figure it uses is present; a denominator that cannot meaningfully divide (zero, negative, missing) makes the ratio missing, not huge; growth needs a positive base a year earlier found by date, not by row. A company with no value for a factor has no view on it, and says so.
- Every stored figure carries a **quality flag** (`ok`, `suspicious_jump`, `identity_error`, ...) and every period says whether its filing date is real or estimated. Flagged figures are treated as missing in research, never repaired silently; the Overview and Data manager count them, and a company's page lists the flagged figures.
- **Survivorship.** Companies that delisted are kept with their losses, and every study reports how many it has. The application never claims a sample is free of survivorship bias unless the securities were declared to be the historical constituents, which a database built from today's tickers is not.
- Derived metrics are persisted with a **calculation version**; a change to a formula bumps it, so old and new numbers are never mixed.

## The factors

Eighty-four factors in thirteen families, each defined in one place with its direction (higher or lower is better, from theory), the figures it reads and a sentence on what it is: **value** (earnings, free-cash-flow, sales, book, EBIT and EBITDA yields, dividend and buyback yield, shareholder yield, a composite), **quality**, **profitability** (margins, ROE, ROA, ROIC), **growth** (revenue, profit, EPS, cash flow, three-year revenue growth, acceleration), **investment** (asset growth, capex, share issuance), **leverage**, **liquidity**, **efficiency** (turnovers, the cash conversion cycle), **earnings quality** (accruals, cash conversion, the accounting anomaly score), **fundamental momentum** (the change and acceleration of margins, returns and leverage), **price momentum**, **risk**, and **size**. The composites are the direction-signed average of their standardised parts; a missing part is not a zero.

A factor passes through a **pipeline** of steps, written as data so a study can be stored, shown and run again: winsorise each date's tails (default 1%), neutralise (remove what the factor shares with a sector, industry, country, the market, size, beta or volatility by a regression on each date), then put the companies on a common scale (z-score, rank or percentile). Every step uses only the cross-section of its own date or the past of its own company.

**On a company's page** (Company explorer → Open): every figure with the day it became public, the ratios built from them, the five-step **DuPont** split of the change in return on equity (tax burden, interest burden, operating margin, asset turnover, equity multiplier) whose contributions add up exactly to the change, the **Piotroski F-score** (nine yes-or-no signs of improving health) and an **accounting anomaly score** of the Beneish kind. The two scores are screens. The anomaly score was fitted on United States manufacturers of the 1980s and early 1990s; a high value means *look closer*, and the application never calls anything fraud on its strength. See [accounting analytics](techniques/accounting-analytics-dupont-piotroski-accruals.md).

## Studying one factor

The **Factor lab** takes a library factor or a formula of your own (`div(fcf,market_cap)`, `delta(roic,12)`, `sector_rank(div(operating_income,total_assets))`), a universe, a way of rebalancing and a way of testing, and returns:

| What | The question it answers |
|---|---|
| **Mean rank IC and its t-statistic**, with a 95% interval | Across companies, does the factor order the next period's returns? The t-statistic and the interval allow for the overlap of the return windows (Newey-West). |
| **Return by bucket** and **monotonicity** | Do the buckets rise in order, or does the whole effect sit in one tail? |
| **Long-short portfolio**, gross and net, with a 95% interval for the Sharpe ratio | Does top-minus-bottom pay after trading costs (default 10 bps one way) and the cost of borrowing the short side (default 50 bps a year)? Sharpe, return, worst fall, turnover. The interval allows for skew and fat tails (Mertens), so a Sharpe ratio is never a bare number. |
| **Decay** | Is the signal still there at three or six months, or gone in a week? It sets how slowly it can be traded. |
| **By year, half, country, sector, region, regime** | Is it steady or the work of a few dates, and does it hold everywhere? |
| **Could this study have seen a real effect?** | Given the number of companies and of dates that do not overlap, the smallest mean IC the study could tell from luck four times in five (its *minimum detectable effect*), and for a true IC of 0.01, 0.02, 0.03, 0.05 and 0.08 the t-statistic it would show and the chance that this t is above 2 and above 3. A study that could not see an IC of 0.03 says so, so a *No clear evidence* from it is not read as *no effect*. |
| **The premium** (Fama-MacBeth) | On every date a regression of the next return on the signal and on size, beta, volatility and momentum (or on controls you name, such as `roic`), then the average slope and its Newey-West error. It says what one standard deviation more of the signal earned over the window with the controls held fixed and without them, and whether holding them fixed took away anything that the per-date difference can tell from noise. The signal enters as the normal score of its rank, so one outlier cannot set the slope. |

The verdict is a sentence with a symbol (✓, ! or ✗), never a colour alone, and the headline, the sentence under it and the first line in the terminal follow one rule: *Strong in this sample* needs |t| of at least 3 and a net Sharpe above 0.5; *Some evidence* needs |t| of at least 2 (a clear signal whose portfolio does not clearly pay is *Some evidence*, and the sentence says why); below that, *No clear evidence*. None of them says validated, and each says so. **Scan every factor** sets all eighty-four side by side under one set of conditions, with a warning that with that many a few show |t| above 2 by luck alone: it is a map of where to look, not a list of alphas. Two factors from the same family are often one idea (see below). See [factor studies](techniques/factor-studies-ic-quantiles-and-decay.md) and [point-in-time fundamentals](techniques/point-in-time-fundamentals.md).

## Searching for alphas

**Alpha discovery** builds formulas as expression trees (a statement figure, a derived ratio, `add sub mul div`, `neg abs log`, `delta growth accel rolling_mean rolling_std` over a number of periods, `rank sector_rank industry_rank zscore`), from the classic formulas of the literature, your own, and random and bred ones, and tests each in stages, cheapest first:

| Stage | A formula must |
|---|---|
| 0 sanity | compute, cover enough of the investable companies, take more than five distinct values on a typical date, and not have been tried before under the same conditions |
| 1 fast IC | have a rank IC with next month's return, on the **training** dates, with a t-statistic past the first-screen bar |
| 2 stability | be positive on enough dates and in both halves, and keep a t-statistic above a bar without its best year |
| 3 quantiles | have buckets that rise in order |
| 4 long-short | earn gross |
| 5 costs | still earn after costs and borrowing, at a turnover that can be traded |
| 6 validation | keep its sign on a second block of dates that stages 1 to 5 never used |
| 7 locked test | (the finalists only, chosen from training and validation) be tested **once** on the last block, which nothing before has looked at |

The dates are cut into three consecutive blocks (default 55%, 20%, 25%) with a gap as wide as the forecast horizon between them, so no return window sits in two blocks. The sign of a formula (whether high or low should earn) is learned on the training block and held fixed afterwards, which makes the test two-sided; the IC figures shown are in that direction, and the library says *higher is better* or *lower is better*.

### The bars

How high each bar sits is a choice, so it has three names, and a run records which it used. **Exploratory** is a first look (the bars the library has always had); **standard** is what the page, the routes and the command line start from; **strict** is for a result you mean to act on. A bar typed by hand replaces the named one, and the verdict of a formula depends on the bars, so a formula that fell short of a strict bar is tried again when the bar is lower.

| Bar | Exploratory | Standard | Strict |
|---|---|---|---|
| Share of investable companies covered | 40% | 50% | 60% |
| Dates in the training block | 36 | 36 | 48 |
| First screen: |t| of the IC | 1.65 | 2.0 | 3.0 |
| Smallest |mean IC| | 0.005 | 0.010 | 0.015 |
| Positive on this share of dates | 55% | 58% | 60% |
| Smallest IC information ratio | 0.10 | 0.15 | 0.20 |
| |t| without its best year | 1.0 | 1.3 | 1.65 |
| Monotonicity of the buckets | 0.5 | 0.6 | 0.7 |
| Long-short |t| and gross Sharpe | 1.5, 0.3 | 1.8, 0.4 | 2.2, 0.5 |
| Net Sharpe | 0.15 | 0.25 | 0.35 |
| Most turnover a rebalance | 70% | 60% | 50% |
| |t| on validation and on the locked dates | 1.0 | 1.3 | 1.65 |
| False-discovery q-value of a finalist, at most | 0.25 | 0.10 | 0.05 |
| The same idea as one already kept, above a correlation of | 0.90 | 0.85 | 0.80 |

Raising a bar after seeing the funnel is choosing it on the answer: choose before the run, and keep the run.

### Before a search: what it could find

*What could this search find?* (and `quant fundamentals preflight`) says what a search of this size could find, before a formula is tried and for nothing. From the number of companies and of dates in each block it gives the smallest true mean IC the first screen catches four times in five, and the locked test; the chance that a real alpha of IC 0.01, 0.02, 0.03, 0.05 or 0.08 gets through the first screen, the validation block, the locked test and all three; and how many formulas luck alone is expected to pass the first screen. A small database says so here, in numbers, rather than by finding nothing.

### What keeps the count honest

- **Every formula that reaches stage 1 is a test and is counted.** The training p-values of all of them go through Benjamini-Hochberg to give each finalist a false-discovery **q-value**; its Sharpe ratio is deflated for the number tried (Bailey and López de Prado); and, with enough survivors, the **probability of backtest overfitting** is estimated by combinatorially symmetric cross-validation. A finalist must also have a q-value within the bar over everything tried.
- **Earlier runs on the same question are counted too.** A search is rarely run once. The ideas tried in other runs on the same *question* (the same universe, rebalance grid, horizon and entry lag, the same pipeline and costs, and the same shares of dates for training and validation) are pooled with this run's: the q-values come from every idea's p-value together and the deflated Sharpe ratio from the pooled count, and a run says how many ideas it counted and from how many runs. An idea tried in two runs is counted once, and ten runs of ten new ideas are a hundred ideas to correct for, not ten.
- **The same idea is never paid for twice.** A rank, z-score or sign on the outside of a formula does not change which companies it prefers, so `neg(rank(x))` and `x` are one idea (a *signal key*); a formula more correlated with one already accepted in the run than the bar allows (0.85 under the standard bars) is the same idea too.
- **The locked dates are counted.** Every look at them is recorded (`oos_looks`), and an alpha says how many looks had been made before its own.
- **Nothing is deleted.** A rejected formula goes to the **graveyard** with the stage it failed at and the reason, under a unique key of formula and conditions; only the metadata of a survivor goes to the library. Ideas tried = alphas in the library + ideas in the graveyard, always.
- **A run can be stopped and resumed exactly.** Every candidate is written the moment it is judged and the stream of formulas is a function of the seed and the position. A finished run cannot be reopened: its locked test has been looked at, and looking again would spend it.
- A complexity penalty in the fitness (information coefficient, stability, Sharpe, monotonicity and unlikeness to the others, less turnover and complexity) prefers the simpler formula when the evidence is equal.

### Does the search find things that are not there?

*Calibrate on shuffled returns* (`quant fundamentals placebo`) runs the same search again, a few times, on copies of the data in which each date's returns have been dealt out to other companies at random. Nothing can predict anything there, whatever the formula, so what gets through is luck, and counting it measures the search's own error rate: how many formulas got through every screen before the locked test (with an exact interval, because it is a small count from a few shuffles), how many of them survived the correction for the number tried, how many runs ended with a Candidate and, set beside a real run (`--run ID`), what share of that run's survivors this makes luck. It also compares the spread of the t-statistics on shuffled returns with 1.00, which is what a correct t-statistic has: a spread above it means the test is too generous on *this* data. The formulas of one search move together under one shuffle (they share the returns that were dealt), so the error of that spread comes from how much it differs from one shuffle to the next, not from how many formulas there are, and it needs at least three shuffles (five are the default); a verdict is given only from 150 formulas upward, and below that, or from fewer shuffles, the answer is "not enough to say". Each date's returns are dealt among the companies that have a return on it, so the pattern of missing values is kept, and the dealing is the same for every horizon.

It is a rehearsal, not a proof. A shuffle destroys the way real signals cluster across companies and dates, so it cannot show the overconfidence that clustering causes; a search that passes it can still be fooled by a market it does not resemble.

### What the library calls an alpha

| Status | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Candidate** | Passed every screen, including dates it was not chosen on. Not trusted. This is the best a search can give. |
| **Validated** | A candidate whose locked-dates t-statistic is at least 1.0 *and* whose evidence survives the correction for the number tried (q-value at most 0.10, or deflated-Sharpe probability at least 0.90). Or one a person approved, with the reason recorded and marked *forced*. |
| **Production** | A validated alpha someone decided to use. |
| **Decaying** | A validated or production alpha whose recent behaviour has weakened (set by the monitor). |
| **Retired**, **Rejected** | No longer used; turned down. Neither can be moved again. |

Moves follow a fixed table (a Candidate can become Validated, Rejected or Retired; Production goes to Decaying, Retired or back to Validated), each needs a reason and is logged. The bars for *Validated* are constants in `src/fundamentals/library.py`; the locked-dates bar in particular is low, because a single look at a short block cannot support more, so treat *Validated* as "not refuted yet", never as "will work". See [automated alpha discovery](techniques/automated-alpha-discovery.md).

### The monitor

An alpha that worked can stop working. The monitor recomputes every alpha that is not retired or rejected on the data now in the database (in the direction it was found) and compares its recent information coefficient (the last twelve months, by default) with the one it earned when it was found. **Healthy**: recent IC at least half the original. **Weakening**: below half. **Decaying**: zero or negative over a year, or under a quarter of the original over two. **Broken**: reversed with evidence (a t-statistic of −2 against the original direction), or covering fewer than 30% of the companies it once covered. A validated or production alpha judged Decaying or Broken is moved to Decaying automatically; nothing is deleted. Fetch new statements and prices first if you want it to see recent months.

## Putting alphas together

Two alphas that rank the companies the same way are one idea, and five copies of one idea should not outvote one different idea. **Alpha library → Correlation** shows the average, over dates, of the rank correlation between each pair (on the companies both cover), the heatmap in the order that puts alike alphas side by side, the clusters (average linkage on 1 − |correlation|), the pairs that are 80% or more alike, and the **effective number of independent ideas** (from the eigenvalues of the correlation matrix: 1 for identical alphas, the number of alphas for unrelated ones).

**Combine** weights the alphas by a rule that uses only what was known on each date: equal weights, their recent IC, their recent IC information ratio, the maximum information ratio (allowing for how their ICs move together), or equal weight to each cluster of alike alphas. **Model** fits one of ridge, lasso, elastic net, random forest, extra trees, gradient boosting, XGBoost or LightGBM *walk-forward* (no training row whose return was still unfolding, with an extra gap if you ask), reports what it relied on (coefficients, the model's own importance, permutation importance and, for the two boosting libraries, SHAP values), and compares the result with the plain rules over the same dates with a paired test. A model that cannot beat a plain average is not earning its complexity; fed pure noise, it is not credited with having beaten the rules. A missing alpha is *no view* (zero), never a bad value. See [combining alphas](techniques/combining-alphas-and-model-combination.md).

## Backtest this alpha

**Backtest** turns a signal (a library alpha, a factor, a formula, a blend, a model) into a portfolio under limits and runs it through the platform's own backtest engine, net of costs, against owning every eligible company equally with a daily rebalance. You choose long-only or long-short, how many names or what percentile each leg holds, the weights (equal, by signal, by inverse volatility), limits (largest position, most net exposure to one sector, industry or country, net beta band, smallest market value and traded value, the largest share of a company's daily trading value one position may be, the most traded at one rebalance), costs and dates.

The limits are met by the nearest book that respects them (a linear programme for how far the book may move toward the wanted one, then a quadratic projection). When they cannot all be met, both legs are scaled down together and the rest stays in cash, and the report says how often. A turnover limit trades part of the way and builds the first book over several dates (the cost of building it is charged). A date with too few eligible names is held in cash and the report says why. The engine earns what the factor tests say the same holdings earn (a test compares the two over every period, to five millionths). The report gives the deflated Sharpe ratio for the number of ideas tried to find the alpha (from the library when it is one), the net-return-against-cost table and its breakeven cost, the result on the dates the alpha was found on and the locked dates it was tested on, how the limits bit, the latest book, and an experiment id that records the signal, the portfolio rules, the universe and the data it ran on. A backtest of a *Candidate* carries a notice that a good backtest is what a search is expected to produce. See [from signal to book](techniques/from-signal-to-constrained-book.md).

And what the tests prove, with known truth: **a placebo world** (each date's returns dealt out to other companies at random, which destroys any relation between a formula and what happened next) yields **no candidates** in two runs of 200 formulas, where the real world yields at least five in one; a world where nothing predicts anything is not made to look otherwise by the number of tries (one seed once let a lucky formula through every screen until the q-value gate was added). The combining rules and the models give **identical earlier weights and predictions when the future returns are altered**. The portfolio is **decided from what was known on each date**. A model fed noise is not credited with beating the rules. The DuPont contributions add up to the change in ROE to rounding. The deflated Sharpe ratio falls with the number of ideas tried. A premium of 0.68% planted in a signal with a skewed spread is recovered (0.72%, t = 7.1) by the premium regression, and a variable that is only a copy of a control has its premium taken away by it.

**The tests were tested.** A script planted 355 small bugs, one at a time, in the research code (an operator, a comparison, a sign or a number changed at random places) and ran the tests: 162 of the 355 changes made a test fail the first time (46%). The survivors were read one by one: many are harmless (a bound that no data touches, a number in a message, a check that repeats one made upstream), and the rest are where the tests did not check what the line does, which is where the exact-value tests of this round were written. The tool is `experiments/plant_bugs.py`.

**Found by using the tab in a browser, then fixed with a test for each:** the monitor flipped an alpha's signal a second time, so an alpha used upside down and still working was judged *Broken*; the library showed the training IC unflipped beside flipped validation and locked figures; the warnings of a blend of several alphas were repeated and did not say which alpha they were about; a count of years under a chart was a share printed as a count; one busy state for the whole tab made a long search block every other view. And in the round that added the checks above: the Overview kept showing the old counts after companies were added or prices loaded until the page was reloaded; the headline over a study could say *Some evidence* while the sentence under it said *Strong in-sample evidence* (one rule now); at a phone's width a wide table could not be reached from the keyboard; a heading skipped a level.

**Not measured:** anything on real companies. No real key has been used. Whether the OpenFilings adapter, the Yahoo symbol mapping and the real filing dates behave as documented is unknown until a first run; the research layer has been tested only on data whose truth is known.

## What is not known

- **Annual only**, so a figure can be up to a year old by the time the next one arrives, and factors that need quarterly figures (earnings surprise, short-term fundamental momentum) cannot be built from real data yet.
- **Restatements** are kept when the provider sends them, but OpenFilings sends the figures as filed; whether a later filing for the same year arrives as a new vintage depends on the provider.
- **Sectors and industries are as of the day they were fetched**, not point in time. A company that changed sector is in its latest sector for the whole history, which can flatter a sector-neutral factor.
- **How many years a request brings back is the provider's, not the application's, and has not been seen on a live key.** The company page says what each request brought back, but the reason a series is short (nothing earlier in the provider's data, or a limit on the length of a series) is not in the documentation and is for the first real run to settle.
- **Yahoo symbols are a best-effort mapping**, delisted companies usually have no history, and prices are in the trading currency (converted with the exchange rate of the date where an amount is set against a price).
- **Survivorship bias remains** in any database built from today's tickers, however many delisted companies it keeps.
- **A backtest is a measurement on a sample.** Costs are a flat number of basis points a side plus a borrow rate, with no market impact beyond the limit on a position's share of daily volume.
- The probability of backtest overfitting needs enough survivors to estimate; with few it is reported as not available.
- **The check against luck is a rehearsal.** Shuffling each date's returns destroys the way real signals cluster, so it can say how often *this search* passes things that cannot be there, not how a market that clusters would fool it. With five shuffles of a few hundred formulas its answers carry wide intervals (they are printed), and the verdict on the t-statistics is withheld below 150 formulas and below three shuffles.
- **The power table assumes the textbook spread.** The smallest effect a study can see uses the spread of an IC across companies that predicts nothing, 1 / sqrt(N - 1), and counts only the dates whose return windows do not overlap. Real cross-sections are clustered (sectors, countries), so the true bar is higher than the printed one, never lower.
- **The premium regression holds fixed only what it is told.** Size, beta (252 days), volatility and momentum come from the prices in the database, not from a risk model, and the regression is linear in them.
- **The bars are my choices.** Three named sets, measured only on a simulated world; on real data they may need to move, and `strict` is a guide for a result you mean to act on, not a guarantee.

## The command line, the routes and the code

`quant fundamentals ACTION` has `status`, `simulate`, `search`, `add`, `plan`, `sync`, `coverage`, `years`, `prices`, `factors`, `evaluate`, `discover`, `preflight`, `placebo`, `library`, `monitor` and `backtest`; `--demo` works in the simulated database and `--json FILE` saves the whole result. `--strictness exploratory|standard|strict` chooses the bars of `discover`, `preflight` and `placebo` (standard from the command line); `placebo` takes `--repeats` (1 to 5 shuffles, 5 by default) and `--run ID` (check a finished run and set what it found beside the result); `evaluate` takes `--premium-controls size beta volatility momentum roic` to choose what the premium regression holds fixed. `coverage COMPANY` lists the years a company holds and `years COMPANY --pick 2005-2012,2015 [--budget N] [--ask-again]` asks for chosen years that are not held (without `--budget` it prints the plan and spends nothing). `quant fundamentals library --validate ID` and `--set-status ID --to Rejected --reason "why"` change a status. A first Ctrl-C asks a running job to stop after its step (what is done is kept); a second ends the command.

The routes behind the tab are under `/api/fund/` (`status`, `overview`, `catalogue`, `companies`, `company`, `coverage`, `quality`, `library`, `alpha`, `graveyard`, `runs`, `run`, `experiments` to read; `companies/add`, `companies/search`, `sync/plan`, `coverage/plan`, `provider/test`, `price-symbol`, `formula/check`, `library/status`, `library/validate` to write; `sync`, `coverage/fetch`, `simulate`, `prices`, `scan`, `evaluate`, `evaluate/formula`, `lookahead`, `discover`, `discover/preflight`, `placebo`, `monitor`, `correlation`, `combine`, `ml`, `backtest` as jobs on the app's worker, and `stop`). Slow work runs as a job, so closing the page does not stop it, and each view of the tab has its own running job, so a long search does not stop you reading the library or a company.

The code is `src/fundamentals` (`schema`, `store`, `providers`, `keys`, `usage`, `service`, `coverage` for the data; `pit`, `metrics`, `analytics`, `research` for what was known when; `factors`, `transforms`, `evaluate` for studies; `expr`, `discovery`, `calibration`, `library` for the search, its check against luck and its memory; `combine`, `bridge` for combinations and the portfolio; `workbench`, `cli`, `report` for what the page and the terminal share), `src/webapp/fundamentals_api.py`, and `src/webapp/static/fundamentals.js` with `fundamentals_lab.js`.

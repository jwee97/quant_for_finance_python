# Free data for the Options, Futures and Crypto tabs

The repository holds no option quotes, no futures contract prices and no multi-exchange crypto quotes, so those three tabs start on a model and say so. Each of them has a **Real data (free)** card in its left panel that downloads the real thing from public sources that need no account and no key (and, for more futures and for a coin's daily history, from iTick, which needs your key; for every contract of 139 futures markets with open interest, from Finzdata, which needs a free key), saves it on your machine and switches the tab to it. You do not find, clean or format anything.

## How to use it

1. Open the Options, Futures or Crypto tab. The card is open while nothing has been downloaded.
2. Tick what you want (tickers, futures roots, coins) and press **Download real data**. A line under the button counts the seconds and a hint beside it estimates the time (ten tickers or roots take a minute or two), because the providers are asked politely, one request after another.
3. The result lists each item with a tick or a cross and the reason for the cross. The tab selects the first download, the badge above every table reads **Downloaded: free data** (or **Mixed sources** when part of the page is still a model, and the parts are listed beside it), and the "How this was made" note names the provider, the time of the download and what the data is not.
4. **Update** repeats a download (the same request, with fresh prices); **Delete** removes it (it asks twice). A futures curve download is merged with the earlier one, so the contracts that have expired since keep their history; an iTick download is topped up with the newest bars, which is one call.

Every download is a file you can open and a `.json` record beside it (`source`, the address, `fetched_at`, `as_of`, the number of rows, the notes, and the request that repeats it). Nothing is written anywhere else: `data/user/options/`, `data/user/futures/` and `data/user/crypto/` are for your own files, which always win over a download of the same name.

## Finding a future by name

Nobody should have to guess a symbol. The Futures card can search both providers' lists:

- **Yahoo Finance** has no page that lists its futures, but its lookup service finds them by name, so the card has a source called *Yahoo: search any future*. It searches a list that ships with the app (about 340 continuous front-month series, `ES=F`, `CL=F`, `6E=F`, in groups the app guesses from the names: energy, metals, grains, livestock, softs, currencies, rates, equity indexes, crypto, single stocks) with no call, and **Ask Yahoo for what you typed** asks Yahoo's lookup (one to three calls) for the rest: `lumber`, `nikkei`, a single contract such as `CLZ26.NYM`. What it finds is kept in `data/user/yahoo_futures.json`, so the next search has it without a call. Every future Yahoo lists is a CME Group market (CME, CBOT, NYMEX, COMEX) or ICE US sugar, coffee, cotton and cocoa: it has no European, Asian or ICE Europe futures. Yahoo's `=F` series is the front contract rolled by Yahoo, not back-adjusted, so the History view lists its largest moves for you to compare with roll dates; it has no open interest, and no curve, roll or carry (those need the per-contract histories of the 44 roots).
- **iTick** publishes its list as the *Product Catalog* spreadsheet in its documentation, which the app bundles (about 1,600 futures products). Its REST API has a product-list call, but it is a lookup by code (`/symbol/list?type=future&region=US&code=ES` needs the `code`), not a listing, and its batch calls (`/future/klines?codes=NQ,ES`) are priced by plan (10 codes on Base, 20 on Professional, 50 on Premium). So there is nothing to page through, and what the bundled list cannot tell is which products *your plan* can download: the finder marks the ones iTick returned (`✓ worked`) or refused (`✗ not for your key`) for your key.

## iTick: any futures product, a coin's history, an underlying's prices

[iTick](https://docs.itick.org/) is the data source of the Backtest tab's *Download other tickers from* menu, and the three cards reuse it: the same key (the `ITICK_API_KEY` secret, see [the dashboard guide](dashboard.md#keeping-the-itick-key-safe)), the same provider, the same limit of calls a minute (the plan's, remembered on disk) and the same refusal to spend more than `ITICK_MAX_CALLS_PER_RUN` calls in one run. A card with iTick chosen and no key shows the steps that turn it on; a request that needs more calls than allowed is refused with its cost before any is spent; a rejected key or plan stops the run at once, with iTick's own words and what they usually mean (a key that neither of iTick's two hosts recognises is not the same as a plan that lacks futures, and "not found this produce" means the code is wrong or the product is not in your plan: see [if iTick rejects the key](dashboard.md#if-itick-rejects-the-key); the finder marks the products your key could and could not get).

What iTick has, and what it does not:

- **Futures: one price series per product, not per contract month.** `NQ` is the E-mini Nasdaq-100 whichever quarter is the front one. The card searches a copy of iTick's own product list (about 1,600 products: 1,381 in the United States, 183 in Hong Kong, 52 in mainland China; the copy is bundled, so searching costs no call), shows the products people look for first, and accepts `US:CODE`, `HK:CODE` or `CN:CODE` typed by hand for one the copy lacks. There is therefore **no curve, roll, carry, basis or open interest** from iTick: the Curve, Roll, Carry and Margin views stay on Yahoo's curves, and an iTick product is read in the **History** view (returns over a day, a month, a quarter and a year, volatility, the worst fall, the calendar months, and the eight largest one-day moves). iTick does not document how its series is joined when one contract expires and the next becomes the front, nor whether its prices are adjusted, so the History view lists the largest moves for you to compare with the roll dates, and the page says that a step that large is often a contract change, not the market.
- **Crypto: a coin's daily closes against USDT, spot only.** iTick has no perpetual prices, funding rates or order books; the exchanges have those. Which exchange's prices its region code stands for is not documented. A coin the exchanges do not list can still be had this way, and iTick's own list of about 220 coins is checked before a call is spent.
- **Options: none.** iTick has no option data at all, and the card says so. What it can add is the underlying's daily prices (Yahoo Finance can too), which give a downloaded chain a realised volatility for any ticker the platform's own price data does not hold; the Options tab then shows realised against implied volatility for it.
- **Never called with a working key by the author.** The requests and answers follow iTick's published documentation, the tests drive the provider through a fake transport, and the client checks what comes back instead of trusting it (bars about a day apart, a history that reaches back as far as asked). What was checked against the live service, without a key: it has `kline` routes for stocks, indices, forex, crypto and `future` (not `futures`) and none for options, it reads the key from a header named `token` (a made-up key is answered "Invalid API key", any other header name "Missing API key"), and the client turns that answer into its plain refusal. What an answer with data looks like is taken from the documentation, not seen. **Test the iTick connection** (in the Backtest tab and, once a key is set, in each card) spends one call and shows what your account returns. The daily bar of a futures product is dated by the exchange's trading day as the app reads it: a bar stamped on the evening before (the day opens at 18:00 in New York) is moved to the next weekday. If your account stamps its bars differently, the dates can be a day off; the History view says nothing about days of the week beyond that.

## Finzdata: every contract of 139 markets, and a window of thirty days

[Finzdata](https://finzdata.com/docs) has a free plan that asks only for an email address and gives a key that begins `fz_`. Its futures data is what the other free sources lack: the daily price, volume and **open interest of every contract month**, expired contracts included, for **139 markets** on CME, CBOT, NYMEX, COMEX, ICE, Eurex, Euronext, SGX and Cboe Futures (equity indexes, interest rates, currencies, energy, metals, agriculture, volatility, crypto). The Futures card has it as a fourth source, *Finzdata: every contract*. The free plan has two limits, and the app is built around them: it shows **only the last 30 days**, and it allows **1,000 calls a day**.

- **Turning it on.** Add the key as a secret named `FINZDATA_API_KEY` (Replit: Tools, then Secrets; on your own computer `export FINZDATA_API_KEY=…` or a line in the git-ignored `.env`; for the weekly check on GitHub, a repository secret of the same name), then stop the app and run it again. Until a key exists the card shows these steps. The key is read when a call is made, sent in one header (`X-API-Key`) to `api.finzdata.com` over HTTPS and nowhere else (a redirect is never followed), and removed from every message, log line and file; the page can learn only whether a key exists. Do not paste it into a chat, an issue, a commit or `.replit`. Finzdata's terms give a key to one person, to be used from one place at a time.
- **Finding the ticker.** The finder under the source searches the list of 139 markets that ships with the app (copied from Finzdata's public page): type a symbol, a name or an exchange (`nasdaq`, `crude`, `eurex`, `E6`), narrow by asset class, and the 43 markets people look for first (the equity indexes, energy, metals, grains, the Treasuries, the main currencies, the VIX, Bitcoin and the main Eurex contracts) come first. Searching costs no call. **Refresh the list from Finzdata (1 call)** replaces the copy with Finzdata's own list, so that its contract counts show and a market it has added appears. Finzdata's symbol is not always the platform's: it writes the euro future `E6` where the platform writes `6E`, and the download maps eight such symbols to the platform's roots, so they take its contract terms and expiry rules. All 44 roots the platform downloads from Yahoo have a Finzdata symbol; the other 95 markets use placeholder contract terms (a multiplier of 1, a tick of 0.01, and the margin share of their asset class), and the notes of the download say so.
- **A download costs two calls a market**: the contract list, then the bars of every contract in one call (if the service insists on one contract at a time, up to 60 calls, the contracts nearest to expiry first). Every call is counted by UTC day in `data/user/finzdata_calls.json`, and the app sends at most 900 a day (`FINZDATA_DAILY_CALLS`) so that a download cannot use the whole allowance; a 429 from the service stops the run at once.
- **See every contract.** *List every contract of the market chosen above* shows each contract of a market with its expiry, whether it still trades, its first and last bar and how many rows are stored here. The expiry is Finzdata's own when it sends one; failing that the day of the last bar of a contract that has stopped trading, then the exchange's rule where the platform has it, then the 15th of the month (marked `~`). A contract that traded past an estimated expiry keeps its rows, with its expiry on the last bar.
- **The window.** Finzdata's free plan answers with the last 30 days whatever you ask, and says so in the answer, which the card repeats. Each download is merged into `data/user/downloads/futures/<ROOT>.csv`, the file the tab already reads for Yahoo's curves, so the file remembers what the plan no longer shows: the first download takes the 30 days, the next asks from a week before the newest stored bar and adds the new days. **The file can only grow from the day you start**: a day that slides out of the window before it is downloaded cannot be fetched again on the free plan. The *Stored from Finzdata* table shows, for each market, the stored span, the number of contracts, whether there is open interest, how old the last bar is, **how many days are left before the window passes it**, and the stretches with no bar. To keep the history whole, press **Choose the stored markets to update** and download within three weeks, or run `python experiments/finzdata_update.py` every day (a cron line, a Replit scheduled deployment, or by hand; `--status` shows the table with no call, and it shares the day's call count with the app).
- **What stays on your machine.** Finzdata's terms forbid publishing its data "in raw or substantially raw form". The stored files and the call count are in `data/user/`, which git ignores and must keep ignoring. The live-feed check keeps nothing and prints counts, dates and column names only.
- **Not verified against the live service.** The paths, parameters and error codes follow Finzdata's OpenAPI document, which leaves the rows of every answer undefined; the readers therefore accept several spellings of each column (`contract` or `symbol`, `expiry` or `expiration`, `close` or `settle`, `open_interest` or `oi`, and so on) and, when one they need is missing, refuse with the columns they were sent. No call has been made with a working key by the author. **The weekly live-feed workflow has two Finzdata checks** (they run when the `FINZDATA_API_KEY` repository secret exists and are skipped without it): `finzdata` downloads the E-mini S&P 500 and crude oil exactly as the card does and checks that the tab can list every contract it got, and `finzdata_reach` reports the plan and the columns of the prices, crypto, options, fundamentals and positioning endpoints. Both print the columns the service really sends. Run the workflow by hand after adding the secret, and a wrong guess can be corrected from one run.
- **The other tabs.** Finzdata also sells daily prices, crypto bars, option end-of-day data and gamma exposure, fundamentals and the CFTC's positioning, but nothing in its OpenAPI document says what a row of those looks like, and the free plan's 30 days is too short for a backtest or a forecast, so only Futures reads it. `finzdata_reach` is how the columns of the rest will be found out.

## What the tabs do with it

- **Options.** The downloaded chain is analysed exactly like a model chain: smile, skew, term structure, surface, Greeks and risk, the strategy builder priced off real quotes. Because a vendor chain gives prices but not the rate and the dividend yield they were struck with, the page assumes the risk-free rate (4% a year, stored with the download) and reads **each expiry's dividend yield from put-call parity at the money**: the forward the market itself implies. That keeps the implied volatilities and the parity checks consistent. Deribit publishes each expiry's forward, which is read directly. The arbitrage view checks only quotes that have a live bid and lie within 5% of the forward for stock and ETF options (15% for the European-style index, Bitcoin and Ether chains): a delayed, wide quote far out of the money, or an in-the-money American option that is worth more than parity says, would otherwise be reported as a violation. Stock and ETF options can be exercised early and are priced as European, which understates the value of that right a little. With the underlying's daily prices downloaded (and at least 130 days of them) the overview shows the 21- and 63-day realised volatility; without them it says so instead of borrowing a number.
- **Futures.** Yahoo's contract histories are stacked into a curve by expiry date. The expiry dates are computed from each exchange's rule (the third-last business day for gold, the third Friday for the equity indexes, the last business day of the month before delivery for heating oil, and so on) without a holiday calendar, so one can be a day or two off. Yahoo's feed has **no open interest**, so the open-interest chart and crossover are left out and say why (Cboe's VX files and Deribit's futures have it). Margins are the illustrative figures of the model for the five roots the platform simulates (CL, NG, GC, HG, ES); for every other root they are **an illustrative share of the contract's value** by asset class (9% for energy, 6% for metals, agriculture and equity indexes, 3.5% for currencies, 2% for rates, 15% for Bitcoin and Ether), labelled as such in the notes: the exchanges set the real figures and change them with volatility. Deribit's futures carry open interest and volume, and so do Cboe's VX files. The History view reads an iTick product (see above). A Finzdata curve has open interest and every contract the service lists, so the open-interest chart and crossover are drawn for it. The curve view lists the nearest 12 contracts unless you choose another number under *Contracts shown*; **every contract the file has** lists all of them (and says how many there are), while the shape and principal-component views keep their nearest eight. A strategy can be backtested on a real curve: [see below](#a-backtest-on-real-curves).
- **Crypto.** A downloaded coin gets its own history, funding and the real venues: the overview, funding, basis and liquidity views read them and say which numbers are data. The cross-exchange view compares venues only within one kind of market (spot with spot, perpetual with perpetual), on real bids and asks at the moment of the download, net of entry-level taker fees that are **assumptions** (they depend on your volume and change). Liquidity walks the real order book (up to 400 levels a side, within 5% of the mid) for the cost of $100 thousand, $1 million and $10 million; a size the saved book cannot fill is shown as n/a, not extrapolated. OKX keeps about three months of funding history whatever you ask. Bitcoin and Ether keep the platform's longer Deribit history; the card adds their venues and funding. A history from iTick is spot only, so the basis and funding views have nothing from it.

## Longer curves: what is free and what is not

Yahoo lists fewer contracts than the exchanges trade, and it drops a contract the day it expires. A free source can therefore be asked for two things: contracts further out, and the contracts that have expired (without which no front month can be followed through the years). What was found, seen from the sandbox the app is developed in on 29 October 2026:

**Building a history of the roots Yahoo has.** Press **Update** on the Futures card now and then (monthly is enough for monthly roots). Nothing else free fills the years before the first download for those roots.

**Looked at and not built.**

- *CME Group's own site* publishes its daily settlement prices, which is where the history of an expired CME contract lives, but a scripted request was answered HTTP 403 ("blocked due to suspected web scraping") and its terms bar automated extraction, so the app does not use it.
- *The EIA* published NYMEX crude oil, natural gas and refined-product futures prices free (contracts 1 to 4, the nearest four delivery months), but says on its tables that futures prices after 5 April 2024 are not available.

- *NSE India's daily "bhavcopy"* (every futures contract of the day, with open interest) answered from the sandbox through the public site that third-party tools read, but NSE sells its end-of-day files as a subscription product, the files hold only the few expiries NSE lists (which Yahoo's depth already beats), and the terms on reuse were not clear enough to build on, so the app does not read them. *HKEX's* historical data and daily reports are licensed services; no free download of its futures reports was found.

- *Interactive Brokers* serves expired futures through its desktop gateway to an account with market data; not built.

## A backtest on real curves

Futures, Backtest, **Prices: Real curves** runs the same strategies as on the simulated roots (carry, trend, breakout, momentum) on the roots you downloaded or put in `data/user/futures/`, through the same pipeline as every backtest here: lagged a day, costed, and scaled to a volatility target when you ask. The file needs the columns `date, contract, expiry, price` (and may have `open_interest`, `volume`, `spot`). What the app does with it:

- **The series.** Each day the book holds the nearest contract that is more than five days from its expiry. The day's return is that contract's price change since the previous close, which is the excess return of rolling it: the roll costs nothing in this series, and its yield shows up as the drift of the contracts. The carry signal is the annualised roll yield of the first two contracts, the slope and basis momentum come from the first three and the first two.
- **Only the front month counts.** A table holding the contracts alive on the day it was fetched (a Yahoo download) has no front month before that day: its nearest contract is years away, and the return of holding it is a deferred contract's. A date counts as front month when the nearest contract is no more than one gap between expiries away, plus the five roll days and ten more. A root with fewer than 252 such days is refused, with the reason, and the page lists the front-month days each root brought. The live Yahoo crude-oil download of 29 October (470 days, 53 contracts) had 5.
- **Weekends and closures.** Weekend rows (Deribit trades every day) are left out, so Monday's return is the move since Friday's close and a year is about 252 returns for every root. A day on which one root's exchange was shut holds its index flat, for at most five days, and never before its first day or after its last.
- **Losing everything.** A leveraged short position can lose more than the whole account in a day. The engine caps that day at -100%, ends the account there (every later day is zero, nothing is traded or charged) and the page says so. Time-series carry on the VIX futures with no volatility target hit it on 5 February 2018; with a 10% target the same strategy lost 33% that year and finished 2015 to October 2026 at about 0% a year (Sharpe 0.06, worst fall 38%). Trend on VX and Bitcoin gave a Sharpe of 0.19. These are one run each on the 29 October download; they show the machinery at work, not a finding.
- **Not modelled.** Margin, funding, the spread paid at the roll, the capacity of each market, and the time between the settlement the files hold and a price you could have traded.
- **Cross-sectional strategies need company.** Cross-sectional carry ranks the roots within an asset class, so it needs two roots of the same class, and basis momentum two commodities; the page refuses a choice that would trade nothing instead of showing a flat line.

## What this data is not

- **Delayed or unaudited, and somebody else's service.** Cboe's quotes are 15 minutes late, Yahoo's last bar can be a session still in progress, and a provider can change a field, refuse an address or disappear. Use the data for research on your own machine; each provider's terms of use are theirs. It is not a feed to trade on, and a backtest on it is still a backtest.
- **Not a history of implied volatility.** A chain is one day. The overview's volatility rank needs a year of implied volatility the downloads do not have, so it says "n/a" instead of borrowing the model's.
- **Not the venues you may expect.** Binance and Bybit refuse US and many cloud addresses (HTTP 451 and 403) and CME's site forbids scripts, so none of them is used.
- **Not the P&L of holding a contract.** An iTick futures history is a price series of a product; Yahoo's curves are the prices of single contracts. Neither includes margin, financing or the cost of rolling, and the series from iTick may step on roll days.
- **Shared cloud hosts get refused.** Yahoo and the exchanges rate-limit addresses that many people share, and Replit's is one of them. When a provider answers "HTTP 403" or "HTTP 429", the card says so by name and the app waits a while before asking that host again instead of hammering it. Try again in a few minutes, download fewer items at a time, or run the dashboard on your own computer.

## Where the code is

`src/assets/free_data/` holds one module per tab (`options.py`, `futures.py` with Yahoo's and Deribit's curves, `cboe_futures.py` for the VIX futures, `crypto.py`), the HTTP client (`http.py`: paced per host, retried with a back-off, a cool-down after a refusal, readable errors), the store (`store.py`), the list of sources (`sources.py`) and iTick's bundled product lists (`itick_catalog.py` with `itick_futures.csv` and `itick_crypto.csv`, copies of iTick's own sheets). Finzdata is `finzdata.py` (the readers and the curve), `finzdata_catalog.py` with `finzdata_futures.csv` (the 139 markets and their search) there, and `src/webapp/finzdata.py` (the client, its key handling and its call ledger) and `src/webapp/data_finzdata.py` (the store: merging, the window, the contract list) on the web side. The iTick downloads themselves are `src/webapp/data_itick.py`, built on the Backtest tab's `ITickProvider` (`src/webapp/providers.py`). The web side is `src/webapp/data_api.py`:

- `GET /api/data/status`: what is downloaded and with which provenance, what each tab can ask for, whether iTick is set up and how many of its calls are free now, and which downloads are running.
- `GET /api/data/search?kind=futures&q=nasdaq&region=US&group=Energy`: products from iTick's bundled list (or `kind=crypto` for coins), best match first. It makes no call.
- `POST /api/data/fetch` (a job; poll `/api/job/<id>`): `{"kind": "options" | "futures" | "crypto", "items": [...], ...settings}`; `"source": "itick"` on a futures or crypto request, `"what": ["chain", "prices"]` on an options one. The browser names a ticker, a root, a coin or a product code; every address and file name is fixed on the server, so a request cannot reach another host or another file. One download per tab at a time, on a worker of its own, so a slow feed never delays a backtest.
- `GET /api/data/contracts?root=ES[&refresh=1]`: every contract Finzdata lists for a market, with its expiry, status and what is stored (one call, or none when the list is under a day old). `POST /api/data/lookup` with `"source": "finzdata"` refreshes the list of markets (one call), `"source": "finzdata"` on a futures fetch downloads and merges, and the status carries the day's calls and the stored markets with the days left in the window.
- `POST /api/data/delete`: `{"kind", "name", "confirm": true}`.
- `GET /api/futures/series?name=US_NQ`: the History view's numbers for a downloaded product.

The tests replace the network with a table of answers in the shape of the live feeds (`tests/free_data_helpers.py`), iTick with a fake service (`tests/fake_itick.py`) and Finzdata with another (`tests/fake_finzdata.py`, whose rows are the likeliest shapes, not a copy of the service's), so none of them goes to the internet. See also [the dashboard guide](dashboard.md), and the guides to [option chains](techniques/option-chains-and-greeks.md), [futures curves](techniques/futures-curves-carry-and-rolls.md) and [crypto funding and venues](techniques/crypto-funding-basis-and-venues.md).
